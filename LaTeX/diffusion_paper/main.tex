\begin{abstract}

Latent diffusion in a pre-existing latent space can preserve the natural manifold structure of chemical spectral data while matching target distributions. Unlike Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) that enforce Gaussian latent distributions through KL divergence regularization, pre-existing latent spaces occupy complex, non-Gaussian manifolds that reflect the intrinsic geometry of the data. This work introduces a geometrically regularized latent diffusion approach tailored to decoupled autoencoded embeddings whose target space is predetermined by ChemNet molecular representations. In practice, synthetic IMS spectra can reduce experimental burden by enabling augmentation, class-balancing, and robustness studies (e.g., instrument variability and low-data regimes) before committing to new collections. Our approach augments standard noise prediction objectives with a centroid separation term and employs a low-noise diffusion schedule designed to retain local manifold geometry while still enabling denoising-based sampling. Using ion mobility spectrometry data across 8 chemicals and 296,692 samples, our approach achieves strong statistical matching (average absolute mean error of 0.08, average relative standard deviation error of 7.4\%) while preserving complex geometric features that naive sampling from a simple, sampleable reference distribution destroys. Downstream evaluation shows that MLP classifiers trained with synthetic augmentation improve in 49 of 50 low-data budget trials at draw scale $\sigma = 1.5$.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Generative modeling in scientific domains faces a fundamental challenge: how to create synthetic data that preserves both statistical properties and physically meaningful structure. In fields like ion mobility spectrometry (IMS)\cite{dodds2019ion}, data collection is expensive, and class imbalance is common, so synthetic generation is attractive. The difficulty is that useful latent representations in this domain are often structured, anisotropic, and class-dependent, rather than simple, easily sampleable isotropic clouds.

Traditional approaches to learned representations, particularly Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), force Gaussian latent distributions through explicit regularization \cite{kingma2014auto}. While this simplifies sampling via $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$, it can overwrite useful geometric information already encoded in the latent space. In contrast, decoupled autoencoders with pre-existing embeddings can exhibit complex, non-Gaussian geometry such as elongated tendrils, clusters, and non-convex class geometry that carry chemically relevant information.

%\subsection{The Manifold Hypothesis and Latent Representations}

Real-world high-dimensional data typically occupies low-dimensional manifolds embedded in latent space \cite{bengio2013representation}. For chemical spectroscopy data, this structure arises from physical constraints: chemical composition, molecular structure, environmental variation, and measurement physics constrain spectra to a much smaller subspace than the full feature dimension suggests.

Our work builds on a \textit{decoupled autoencoder} architecture \cite{dunham2024oracle} that makes this issue explicit. Rather than learning latent codes from scratch, we fix the latent space to be pre-trained ChemNet molecular embeddings \cite{preuer2019frechet}. The encoder maps 1676-dimensional IMS spectra into this predetermined 512-dimensional chemical embedding space, and the decoder maps back to spectra. Because ChemNet embeddings were not trained to satisfy a simple, sampleable prior (e.g., Gaussian), the resulting latent geometry is strongly non-Gaussian and often non-convex.% geometry, a direct consequence of the architectural choice to use chemically-meaningful, pre-defined representations rather than imposing distributional assumptions for sampling convenience.

The resulting latent space exhibits non-Gaussian geometry with elongated structures, tendrils, and clusters reflecting chemical similarity relationships. Chemical clustering occurs naturally, with distinct separation between chemical classes and smooth transitions within classes. Different chemicals occupy varying extents of latent space, reflected in per-chemical principal component explained variance ratios that range from highly linear (background simulant) to broadly distributed (Simulant D).

VAE-style Gaussian regularization, while enabling simple sampling, destroys this structure and is incompatible with making use of known latent information. Forcing $q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ through KL divergence penalties flattens the manifold, losing the geometric relationships that encode physical meaning.

%\subsection{Diffusion in the Latent Space}

Recent diffusion models provide a practical alternative: instead of constraining the latent distribution, learn to sample from the distribution that is already there \cite{ho2020denoising}. Training diffusion directly in latent space allows generation while preserving manifold geometry.

A key advantage of diffusion models is their ability to gradually transform structured, non-Gaussian latent data into a diffusion-space distribution that is easier to sample from, then map those samples back through reverse denoising. Figure~\ref{fig:ho-diffusion} shows this progression for Simulant A latents under our $\beta_{\max}=0.02$ schedule: the clean latent distribution at $t=0$, intermediate noisy states, and the maximally diffused state at $t=49$. This progression motivates drawing at high diffusion time and using the learned reverse process to recover valid ChemNet-space points.

%\subsection{Contributions}

We present a diffusion-based generation method for \emph{pre-existing} latent spaces, where the objective is to match an existing distribution without erasing its geometry. The method is designed for decoupled autoencoder settings in which latent coordinates already encode domain knowledge and should be preserved.

We introduce latent diffusion with a joint objective (80\% noise prediction, 20\% centroid-separation regularization) and class-conditioning on molecular embeddings. This combination keeps the inter-class structure from collapsing while still learning a global denoising map that supports generation for multiple chemicals in a single model.

We also show that a low-noise schedule ($\beta_{\max}=0.02$) is an effective operating point for this problem: it introduces enough stochasticity to support diffusion-based sampling while avoiding aggressive corruption that can blur latent geometry. The model matches target statistics with mean absolute error 0.08 and 7.4\% relative standard deviation error across 8 chemicals, with MLP classifiers improving in 49 of 50 low-data trials at $\sigma = 1.5$.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Diffusion Models for Generation}

Diffusion models have emerged as powerful generative frameworks, learning to reverse gradual noising processes through iterative denoising. Sohl-Dickstein et al. \cite{Sohl-DicksteinW15} introduced the foundational connection to non-equilibrium thermodynamics, while Ho et al. \cite{ho2020denoising} demonstrated that denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) could achieve state-of-the-art generation quality through noise prediction objectives. These models have since been extended to various domains, including text-to-image generation \cite{ramesh2021zero}, where their ability to handle complex, non-convex data manifolds proves advantageous.

While most diffusion work focuses on pixel-space generation, our approach operates in latent space with pre-existing embeddings, requiring explicit geometric preservation that standard diffusion objectives do not guarantee.

\subsection{Manifold Learning and Latent Representations}

The manifold hypothesis \cite{bengio2013representation} posits that high-dimensional data occupies low-dimensional manifolds embedded in observation space. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) \cite{kingma2014auto} learn such representations through encoder-decoder architectures, but impose Gaussian priors via KL divergence regularization. While this enables straightforward sampling, it can destroy natural geometric structure when the 
data manifold is inherently non-Gaussian.

Invertible neural networks \cite{Ardizzone2019INN, Ardizzone2019GIG} provide an alternative by learning bijective mappings between data and latent spaces, preserving geometric relationships without distributional constraints. Our decoupled autoencoder approach similarly avoids Gaussian regularization, instead fixing the latent space to pre-existing molecular embeddings.

\subsection{Chemical Data Generation and Domain Applications}

Molecular generation has motivated specialized embedding methods that encode chemical structure. ChemNet \cite{preuer2019frechet} provides deep molecular embeddings trained on millions of chemical structures, capturing similarity relationships in SMILES representations. These embeddings serve as our fixed latent space, bringing domain knowledge into the generation process.

For ion mobility spectrometry specifically \cite{dodds2019ion, hill1990ion}, data scarcity motivates synthetic generation, but preserving physically meaningful structure is critical. Recent work on decoupled autoencoders \cite{dunham2024oracle} demonstrates that fixing latent spaces to molecular embeddings can improve chemical detection, providing the architectural foundation we build upon for diffusion-based generation.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Ion Mobility Spectrometry}

Ion mobility spectrometry (IMS) is an analytical technique that separates ionized molecules based on their velocity in an electric field \cite{hill1990ion, dodds2019ion}. The resulting spectra measure ion intensity as a function of drift time, providing chemical fingerprints for identification and detection applications. IMS offers rapid analysis times (milliseconds) with high sensitivity, making it particularly valuable for chemical detection in security, environmental monitoring, and defense applications \cite{dodds2019ion}.

Our IMS dataset comprises 296,692 samples across eight real-world chemical simulants, including one disruptive background simulant.%: DEB (Simulant A), DEM (Simulant B), DMMP (Simulant C), DPM (Simulant D), DtBP (Simulant E), JP8 (Jet fuel, background simulant), MES (Simulant F), and TEPO (Simulant G). 
Each sample consists of 1676 features%(838 drift time measurements for positive ions, 838 for negative ions)
, split into 222,519 training/validation and 74,173 test samples.

\subsection{Decoupled Autoencoders with Chemical Embeddings}

Following previous work \cite{dunham2024oracle}, we employ a \textit{decoupled autoencoder} where the latent space is \textbf{given ahead of time} rather than learned. This is a critical architectural choice that fundamentally shapes the manifold structure we observe.

In standard autoencoders, the latent space is learned end-to-end from reconstruction objectives, with no constraints on its geometry. VAEs impose Gaussian structure through explicit KL regularization. Our decoupled approach takes a third path: we \textit{fix} the latent space to be the 512-dimensional ChemNet embeddings \cite{preuer2019frechet}, pre-trained on 3.6 million chemical structures to encode molecular SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) representations.

The encoder $E$ learns to map IMS spectra $\mathbf{x}_c$ for chemical $c$ to this \textit{pre-existing} ChemNet embedding space:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{enc}} = \|\mathbf{h}_c - E(\mathbf{x}_c)\|^2
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_c \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$ is the fixed ChemNet embedding for chemical $c$.

The decoder $D$ learns the inverse mapping, reconstructing spectra from ChemNet embeddings:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{dec}} = \|\mathbf{x}_c - D(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_c)\|^2
\end{equation}

This decoupled architecture is critical to our approach. Since the latent space is predetermined by ChemNet's training on molecular structures, with no Gaussian distributional constraints, there is \textbf{no guarantee that the resulting latent distribution is sampleable}. Indeed, as Figure~\ref{fig:per-chemical-pca} demonstrates, the ChemNet embeddings for IMS chemicals exhibit highly non-Gaussian, tendril-like structures with non-uniform variance. This pre-existing, non-Gaussian latent geometry is the foundational motivation for our diffusion approach, as we cannot assume Gaussian sampling will work when the latent space itself is not Gaussian by construction.

The decoupled autoencoder thus provides a rich, chemically-informed 512-dimensional latent space that respects molecular structure relationships while exhibiting the complex manifold geometry that standard generation methods fail to capture.

\subsection{Diffusion Models}

Diffusion models learn to reverse a gradual noising process, drawing inspiration from non-equilibrium thermodynamics \cite{Sohl-DicksteinW15}. The forward process adds Gaussian noise over $T$ time steps:
\begin{equation}
q(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{x}_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I})
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t (1-\beta_s)$ controls the noise schedule.

Traditional diffusion models parameterize the reverse process to predict the noise $\epsilon$ added at each step \cite{Sohl-DicksteinW15, ho2020denoising}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_0,\epsilon}[\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\|^2]
\end{equation}

While this standard formulation excels at learning data distributions, it does not inherently preserve geometric structure when multiple classes occupy distinct regions of the manifold. Our work addresses this by augmenting noise prediction with explicit geometry-preserving objectives and by using a lower-noise schedule than typical image diffusion settings.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Geometry-Preserving Diffusion in Latent Space}

Our approach applies noise prediction diffusion directly to the 512-dimensional latent space learned by the ChemNet-informed decoupled autoencoder, augmented with a centroid-separation regularizer that explicitly preserves the inter-class geometric structure.

\subsubsection{Architecture}

The denoiser is a six-layer MLP with 512 hidden units and SiLU activations\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/ElfwingUD17}, taking as input the concatenation of the noised latent $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$, time embedding $t_{emb} \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$, SMILES embedding $s \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$, and class one-hot $c \in \mathbb{R}^8$, and outputting predicted noise $\hat\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$.

The time embedding uses sinusoidal positional encodings followed by a two-layer MLP, providing rich temporal context to the denoising network.

\subsubsection{Training Objective}

The diffusion schedule uses a linear beta schedule over $T=50$ time steps:
\begin{equation}
\beta_t = \beta_{\text{min}} + (t-1) \cdot \frac{\beta_{\text{max}} - \beta_{\text{min}}}{T-1}
\end{equation}
with $\beta_{\text{min}} = 0.001$ and $\beta_{\text{max}} = 0.02$. This low-noise regime was selected because it preserves local latent geometry while still moving samples toward a denoising-friendly diffusion space. In contrast, much larger terminal noise (e.g., $\beta_{\text{max}} \approx 0.2$ in many settings) can over-corrupt fine structure in this application. At $\beta_{\text{max}} = 0.2$, the forward process would corrupt the latent space by a magnitude comparable to the inter-class standard deviations reported in Table~\ref{tab:ims-statistics}, erasing the tendril structure the model is meant to reproduce.

Training samples uniformly over time steps $t \sim \text{Uniform}(0, T-1)$ and optimizes a combined objective. The noise-prediction term follows the standard DDPM formulation:
\begin{align}
\epsilon &\sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I}), \\
\mathbf{x}_t &= \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon, \\
\hat{\epsilon} &= \epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{c}), \\
\mathcal{L}_{\text{noise}} &= \|\epsilon - \hat{\epsilon}\|^2.
\end{align}

To preserve inter-class geometric structure, the centroid-separation regularizer operates on predicted clean latents and enforces minimum distances between class centroids:
\begin{align}
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 &= \frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\hat{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}} \\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_c &= \frac{1}{|B_c|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in B_c} \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0,i} \quad \text{(class centroids)} \\
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sep}} &= \frac{1}{C(C-1)}\sum_{i \neq j} \max(0, m - \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|_2)
\end{align}
where $B_c$ denotes samples of class $c$ in the batch, $C=8$ is the number of classes, and $m=5.0$ is the margin hyperparameter. The margin value was selected based on preliminary experiments to approximate one standard deviation of the inter-class centroid distances, balancing separation enforcement with flexibility for within-class variation.

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{noise}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{noise}} + \lambda_{\text{sep}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{sep}}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\text{noise}} = 0.8$ and $\lambda_{\text{sep}} = 0.2$. This 80/20 weighting balances generative quality with geometric preservation. Systematic ablation of these hyperparameters is left for future work.

We use AdamW optimization with learning rate $5 \times 10^{-5}$, weight decay $0.01$, batch size 256, and train for up to 1000 epochs with early stopping (patience=100 epochs). The learning rate follows a cosine annealing schedule.

\subsubsection{Sampling via DDPM}

We employ standard DDPM sampling \cite{ho2020denoising}, iteratively denoising from Gaussian noise. Starting from $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$, we reverse the diffusion process over all $T=50$ time steps:

\begin{align}
\hat{\epsilon}_t &= \epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{s}, \mathbf{c}), \\
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 &= \frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\hat{\epsilon}_t}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}, \\
\mathbf{x}_{t-1} &= \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\hat{\epsilon}_t + \sqrt{\beta_t}\mathbf{z},
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ for $t > 0$ and $\mathbf{z} = 0$ at the final step.

The relatively small number of time steps (50 vs. 1000 in typical DDPM implementations) provides efficient sampling while maintaining sufficient expressivity for the 512-dimensional latent space.

\subsection{Class-Conditioned Multi-Chemical Generation}

Rather than training separate models per chemical, we condition a single diffusion model on both molecular structure (SMILES embeddings) and chemical identity (one-hot class labels). This approach achieves parameter efficiency by handling all 8 chemicals with a single model trained on 222,519 samples. Common chemical features are learned once and reused across related chemicals. Adding new chemicals requires only their SMILES embeddings and class labels, without retraining from scratch.

The conditioning concatenates molecular information with noised latents, allowing the model to adapt its predictions based on the target chemical's identity and structure.

\subsection{Role of Geometry Regularization}

To validate the importance of explicit geometry regularization, we compare our approach to standard noise prediction diffusion without the centroid-separation term (i.e., $\lambda_{\text{sep}} = 0$). Without this regularizer, the model can match first and second-order statistics while losing the inter-class geometric structure: generated samples from different chemicals blend together in latent space, reducing the distinct clustering that reflects chemical similarity relationships.

The centroid-separation regularizer addresses this by explicitly encouraging class centroids to maintain minimum distances during training. This margin-based objective ($m=5.0$) provides gradients that guide the model to respect natural class boundaries while still learning within-class variation. \textit{Geometric structure preservation requires explicit geometric supervision}; matching statistical metrics alone does not guarantee manifold faithfulness.

By combining 80\% noise prediction loss with 20\% geometry regularization, we achieve a balance: the noise prediction term ensures high-quality generation and distributional matching, while the regularizer preserves the geometric structure that makes different chemicals distinguishable in latent space. VAE regularization enforces $q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ through KL divergence \cite{kingma2014auto}, destroying non-Gaussian structures visible in Figure~\ref{fig:per-chemical-pca}.

\subsection{Controlled Draws in Diffused Space}

To control synthetic diversity at sampling time, we performed a draw-variance sweep in the diffused space and decoded the resulting latents to spectra. Specifically, we generated synthetic sets using Gaussian draw scales $\sigma \in \{1.0, 1.5, 2.0\}$, corresponding to the datasets later evaluated as std1.0, std1.5, and std2.0.

Figure~\ref{fig:batched-std-pca} shows how this draw scale changes the generated support relative to real data. Lower draw scale ($\sigma=1.0$) yields conservative samples concentrated near dense manifold regions, while higher scales ($\sigma=1.5, 2.0$) increase spread and introduce broader coverage. This controlled stochasticity provides a practical knob for balancing fidelity and diversity without retraining the model.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Geometry vs. Statistics as Generation Targets}

Without the centroid-separation regularizer, the model matches first- and second-order statistics while generated samples from distinct chemicals drift toward each other in latent space, as confirmed by the PCA projections. The comparison between our geometry-preserving latent diffusion setup and standard diffusion without geometric constraints reveals that \textit{how} statistical moments are matched matters as much as \textit{that} they are matched.

VAE-style Gaussian regularization represents the opposite extreme: by forcing $q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$ through KL divergence penalties, we gain sampling convenience at the cost of destroying natural manifold structure. Our approach maintains this structure by augmenting standard diffusion with explicit geometric objectives.

%\subsection{Geometry Regularization During Training}

The superior performance of this approach stems from explicit geometric supervision during training. Standard noise prediction models learn to reverse the diffusion process:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_\theta: \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,T] \times \mathcal{C} \to \mathbb{R}^d
\end{equation}
mapping noisy latents, time steps, and conditions $\mathcal{C}$ like temperature and pressure to noise predictions. Without geometric constraints, this objective can produce samples that match statistical moments while collapsing the inter-class structure.

Our separation loss provides complementary supervision by operating on the predicted clean latents:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{sep}}: \{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0^{(i)}, c_i\}_{i=1}^{B_c} \to \mathbb{R}^+
\end{equation}
penalizing insufficient distance between class centroids. This margin-based constraint ($m=5.0$) encourages the model to maintain distinct chemical clusters while learning distributional structure. The combined objective ($80\%$ noise + $20\%$ separation) balances generative quality with geometric fidelity.

\subsection{Implications for Low-Data Scientific Domains}

In domains like IMS, where data acquisition is expensive, decoupled autoencoders learn rich, geometrically meaningful representations without distributional constraints; VAE-style Gaussian regularization is unnecessary. Augmenting standard diffusion with explicit geometric objectives, such as centroid-separation regularization, preserves manifold structure that statistical objectives alone cannot guarantee. A single class-conditioned diffusion model can generate for multiple related chemicals, leveraging shared structure while maintaining chemical-specific features through separation guidance.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

While our approach achieves strong results on IMS data, several directions warrant investigation. Some chemicals, particularly Simulant G, exhibit incomplete manifold coverage (Figure~\ref{fig:per-chemical-pca}), likely due to lower training representation or complex geometry. Adaptive sampling or chemical-specific schedules could improve coverage. 

Systematic ablation of $\lambda_{\text{noise}}$, $\lambda_{\text{sep}}$, and margin $m$ would quantify trade-offs between generative quality and geometric preservation. Our current choices (0.8, 0.2, 5.0) were selected empirically. Quantitative comparison against VAE baselines would empirically validate Section~4.4's theoretical arguments about geometric degradation under Gaussian regularization.

Future extensions including uncertainty quantification for selective augmentation, conditioning on continuous chemical properties, multi-run confidence intervals, and theoretical analysis of geometry-preservation conditions would strengthen the approach.

Concretely, three gaps remain: incomplete manifold coverage for Simulant G, no systematic ablation of $\lambda_{\text{noise}}$, $\lambda_{\text{sep}}$, and $m$, and classifier results reported without multi-seed confidence intervals.

\section{Conclusion}

Latent diffusion in a pre-existing embedding space preserves the natural manifold structure of chemical spectral data while matching target distributions. Applied to ion mobility spectrometry data across 8 chemicals and 296,692 samples, our approach achieves strong statistical matching while maintaining complex geometric features that naive sampling from a simple, sampleable reference distribution destroys.

Learned representations naturally occupy non-Gaussian manifolds reflecting intrinsic data structure. We show that augmenting standard diffusion with explicit geometric supervision, via a margin-based centroid-separation regularizer, preserves the inter-class structure while enabling within-class variation. By combining 80\% noise prediction with 20\% geometry regularization, we achieve a principled balance between generative quality and geometric fidelity.

For low-data scientific domains where preserving physical structure is as important as matching statistical moments, MLP classifiers augmented with synthetic data improve in 49 of 50 low-data budget trials, confirming that geometry-preserving generation translates to practical gains when labeled data is scarce.
